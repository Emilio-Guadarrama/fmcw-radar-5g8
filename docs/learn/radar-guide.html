Learning guide · fmcw-radar-5g8 · status Nov 2026

SPECULA-1, explained simply

A small, cheap radar that measures how far away things are, using radio waves around 5.8 GHz. This page walks through how it works, what is in the repository, and where the project stands, with an analogy for every hard word.

This guide is for learning. The numbers are design targets (assumptions) or calculated values from the simulation, not measurements. The source of truth is docs/requirements.md and the decisions in docs/decisions/. Interactive charts are simplified illustrations, not the project's simulation.

The whole project in one sentenceIt works like a bat. A bat squeaks and listens for the echo. This radar "squeaks" a radio tone whose pitch slides upward, listens for the echo, and works out the distance from how far the echo's pitch lags behind.
Part 1 · The core idea

FMCW: the sliding whistle trick

FMCW stands for Frequency-Modulated Continuous Wave. In plain words: the radar transmits all the time (continuous wave), and it keeps changing its frequency (frequency-modulated) in a straight upward ramp called a chirp.

Analogy: singing in a canyonImagine you sing a note that rises smoothly from low to high, and you keep sliding. Your voice bounces off a cliff and comes back a moment later. When it returns, you are already singing a higher note than the echo. The difference between the note you are singing and the note you hear is bigger when the cliff is further away. That difference is the beat frequency, and it is the number the radar measures.

The gap between the lines is exaggerated so you can see it. In reality the echo from 30 m comes back after only 200 nanoseconds, which is 0.02 % of the 1 ms chirp.

The one formula that matters

beat frequency  f_b = 2 · R · S / c

R = distance to the target (m)
S = slope = how fast the pitch climbs = B / T = 115 MHz / 1 ms = 1.15 × 10¹¹ Hz per second
c = speed of light ≈ 300 000 km/s
2 = the wave goes there AND back

Plug in 30 m and you get about 23 kHz. That is a sound-like frequency, slow enough for a cheap computer chip to record. This is the magic of FMCW: a 5.8 billion Hz radio problem turns into a 23 thousand Hz audio-like problem.

Part 3 · The software brain

The FFT: a prism for signals

The mixer output is a messy wiggle. If there are three objects, it contains three tones on top of each other. The FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) is an algorithm that splits a signal into the tones inside it.

Analogy: prism or music equalizerA prism splits white light into colors. The bouncing bars on a music equalizer show how much bass, mid and treble is playing. The FFT does the same for the beat signal. Each bar ("bin") is a distance slot, and a tall bar means "something is there".

Range spectrum for the target from Part 1

Ideal simulation: one target, Hann window, no noise. Each bar is one range bin about 1.3 m wide at 115 MHz. Move the slider in Part 1 and the peak moves.

Part 4 · Resolution

Why "1.3 m" doesn't mean you can see two things 1.3 m apart

Range bin = c / (2B). With B = 115 MHz that is 1.30 m. Wider sweep → narrower bins. But each target makes a "hill" in the spectrum, not a thin line, and two hills close together melt into one. The project's simulation found two equal targets need about 2.0 m apart to show up as two peaks, so the official goal is 2.5 m or less.

Analogy: two car headlights at nightFar away, a car's two headlights blur into one blob. As it gets closer, you can tell there are two. Resolution is the distance at which "one blob" becomes "two lights".

Try it: two targets near 20 m

Illustration only: 115 MHz, ideal, two equal echoes in phase. The project's real test checks 72 different phase combinations, which is why its numbers are a bit stricter.

The window is a fade-in/fade-out applied to each chirp before the FFT. Rectangular (no fade) gives sharp hills but noisy "side lobes" that can hide a weak target next to a strong one. Hann (smooth fade) gives clean, wider hills. The project uses rectangular only for the two-target test and Hann for everything else.

Analogy: camera focusRectangular is a sharp lens with lens flare. Hann is a slightly soft lens with no flare.
Part 5 · The legal question

Why 115 MHz and not 150 MHz

Radio frequencies are like lanes on a public highway: you need permission to drive in most of them. Mexico's regulator lists 5725–5850 MHz as "free spectrum". The original plan swept up to 5875 MHz, 25 MHz outside the listed lane. So the team chose decision 001:

  • 5 MHz guard on each edge so the real signal, which is never perfect, doesn't spill out of the lane.
  • Cost: range bin grows from 1.0 m to 1.3 m.
  • Nothing has been cleared to transmit. Open item M-01: it is still unconfirmed whether an FMCW radar qualifies for that free band. A university project is not an exemption. Until this is resolved, tests use only cables and a dummy load (a plug that safely swallows the signal instead of radiating it).
Part 6 · Goals

What "success" means (decision 002, "Balanced")

All of these are targets, not proven results. No hardware exists yet.

GoalValueIn plain words
Range3–30 mWorks for objects between 3 and 30 m away, indoors.
Two-target resolution≤ 2.5 mTwo equal objects 2.5 m apart show as two peaks.
Accuracy≤ 0.20 m, 95 %Distance is within 20 cm in 95 of 100 tries, when the echo is clear (SNR ≥ 20 dB).
DetectionPd ≥ 0.90 at Pfa 1e-4Finds a real target at least 90 % of the time, and raises a false alarm in at most 1 of 10 000 empty slots.
Update rate≥ 10 HzAt least 10 fresh readings per second (16 chirps averaged per frame).
Latency≤ 200 msThe screen lags reality by at most a fifth of a second.
Reference target15 cm corner reflectorA three-sided metal corner that bounces radio straight back, like a bike reflector does with light.
Analogy: smoke alarm for Pd and PfaPd (probability of detection) is how often the alarm rings when there really is smoke. Pfa (probability of false alarm) is how often it rings when you are only making toast. Set the threshold lower and you catch more real fires, but you also get more false alarms. The live display puts its threshold 11 dB above the measured noise to hit Pfa = 1e-4.

The clever part: source.py defines a "plug shape". The simulator fits it today; when the real hardware exists, someone writes a USB version with the same shape and the rest of the software doesn't change. Like a phone charger port: swap the charger, keep the phone.

Part 9 · House rules

How to contribute without breaking anything

  1. Read docs/charter.md first.
  2. Label every number as datasheet, calculated, measured or assumption, always with units. Never invent a spec or price.
  3. Keep simulation, bench tests and live radiation clearly separate. Nothing gets radiated yet.
  4. Decisions go in docs/decisions/NNN-title.md; gate reviews in docs/gates/Gn.md.
  5. Python 3.11+, pytest tests for every module, formatted with ruff. Small commits, imperative messages ("Add X", not "Added X").
  6. No AI attribution lines in commits. Link datasheets; don't commit the PDFs.

Glossary

Every hard word, one line each

GHz / MHz / kHz
Billions / millions / thousands of vibrations per second.
Chirp
One sweep of rising frequency, here 1 ms long.
Sweep bandwidth (B)
How far the chirp climbs: 115 MHz. Wider = finer distance detail.
Slope (S)
How fast it climbs: B divided by chirp time.
Beat frequency
The difference between what's sent now and the echo. Proportional to distance.
TX / RX
Transmit / receive.
LO (local oscillator)
The copy of the chirp fed to the mixer for comparison.
Baseband
The slow signal after mixing (kHz), easy to record.
Sample rate (kSPS)
Thousand samples per second the ADC takes.
Nyquist / aliasing
You can only record tones up to half the sample rate. Higher ones disguise themselves as lower ones, like wagon wheels spinning backwards in films.
Range bin
One distance slot in the FFT output, c/(2B) wide.
Window (Hann, rectangular)
Fade applied before the FFT; trades sharpness for cleanliness.
Side lobes
Small fake bumps beside a real peak.
SNR / dB
Signal-to-noise ratio; decibels are a log scale where +10 dB = 10× more power.
Noise floor
The background hiss level everything has to rise above.
Threshold / CFAR
The "loud enough to count" line. CFAR sets it automatically from the measured noise.
Waterfall
A scrolling picture of the spectrum over time.
Doppler
Pitch shift from motion (an ambulance passing). Velocity is a later goal, not v1.
EIRP
Effective radiated power: transmitter power times antenna focus. Legal limits use it.
ISM band
Radio "lanes" set aside for industrial, scientific and medical use, like Wi-Fi's.
Dummy load
A plug that absorbs the signal so nothing is broadcast.
VNA
Vector Network Analyzer: tests how well antennas and cables pass signals. Must reach 6 GHz here.
S11
How much signal bounces back from an antenna instead of going out. Lower is better.
BOM
Bill of materials: the shopping list of parts.
PCB / schematic / layout
Circuit board / the wiring diagram / where parts and copper traces physically go.
Altium / KiCad / openEMS
Board design programs, and an antenna simulator.
Firmware
The program that runs on the ESP32 chip.
Link budget
An accounting sheet of signal gains and losses on the round trip.
Radar cross-section
How "big" an object looks to the radar, in m².
CRT (formerly IFT)
Mexico's telecom regulator.
